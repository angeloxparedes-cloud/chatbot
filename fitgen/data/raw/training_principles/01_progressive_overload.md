# Progressive Overload

Progressive overload is the gradual increase of stress placed on the body during
exercise training. It is the single most important principle for continued
strength and muscle gains.

## Core Methods
1. **Increase load** – add weight to the bar or dumbbells once you can complete
   all prescribed reps with good form for two consecutive sessions.
2. **Increase reps** – add 1–2 reps per set before increasing load.
3. **Increase sets** – add an additional working set to a lift.
4. **Increase frequency** – train a muscle group or movement pattern more
   often per week.
5. **Decrease rest time** – shorten rest between sets to increase density
   (best for hypertrophy/conditioning phases, not strength phases).
6. **Improve range of motion / tempo** – slower eccentrics or fuller depth
   increase difficulty without changing the external load.

## Guidelines for Beginners
- Beginners can often add weight every session for the first 4–8 weeks
  ("linear progression").
- Do not increase load if form breaks down. Technique takes priority over
  numbers on the bar.
- A rough target: increase load by 2.5–5% once the top of a rep range is hit
  for all sets.

## Guidelines for Intermediate/Advanced Lifters
- Progression slows down; use weekly or bi-weekly increases instead of
  session-to-session.
- Periodization (cycling volume and intensity across weeks) becomes
  necessary to keep progressing and to manage fatigue.
- Deload weeks (reduced volume/intensity every 4–8 weeks) help prevent
  plateaus and overtraining.

## Common Mistakes
- Increasing load too fast, leading to form breakdown and injury risk.
- Only tracking weight lifted and ignoring reps, sets, and rest — all four
  variables matter.
- Never deloading, which leads to accumulated fatigue and stalled progress.
